Introduction to Gaussian process. Related to Figure 1.
In this section we provide a basic introduction to Gaussian Process, and discuss the mapping
between the thresholded Gaussian process and a simplified picture of neural dynamics.
Gaussian processes can be viewed as a generalization of a multivariate Gaussian variable,
having a probability density function of the form

multivariate variable to have the form of Eq. S10, and can therefore be used as a definition of a
multivariate Gaussian distribution, instead of Eq. S10.
It is possible to generalize the concept of a Gaussian distribution over vectors to a Gaussian
distribution over functions of a continuous variable x ∈[0, L]: the variable x can be thought of as
a continuous analogue of the discrete index that labels the elements of a random vector. More
formally a random distribution over functions f(x) is a Gaussian process if for every discrete set
of k points, the values of the function (f(x1), · · · , f(xK)) are jointly distributed as a multivariate
Gaussian.
Similarly to the discrete case, where the vector m and the covariance matrix C
uniquely determine the distribution of a multivariate Gaussian variable, a Gaussian process is
uniquely determined by its mean function
m(x) = ⟨f(x)⟩,
and its (two-variable) covariance function
r(x, y) = ⟨[f(x) −m(x)] [f(y) −m(y)]⟩.
In this work we consider stationary Gaussian processes, having the property that r(x, y) is func-
tion of x −y and can thus be written as r(x −y). In addition, the mean m(x) of a stationary
Gaussian process is independent of x, and is assumed in this work to vanish. Note, specifically,
that the variance of the process is constant throughout the space and is given by r(0).
In our model, the underlying index x ∈[0, L] represents the location of the animal in its
environment, and the turning curve of a neuron is obtained by applying a threshold nonlinearity
to its associated instance of the Gaussian process. A simple biological interpretation of the
model is that the Gaussian process represents the mean synaptic input that the neuron receives
from its presynpatic partners, whose activities are themselves spatially tuned. The threshold-
linear transfer function, which relates the total synaptic input to the cell’s firing rate arises from
the interplay of the synaptic input, the intrinsic dynamics of the neuron, and noise S6. Technically,
in the theory of Gaussian processes, the regions in which the process exceeds a threshold are
called excursion sets, and these are mapped to place fields in our model.
According to the model, at any fixed position of the animal the synaptic activation is distributed
as a Gaussian variable across the neural population, and so is the synaptic activation of a single
neuron across positions. This gives rise to a thresholded Gaussian distribution of firing rates.
Interestingly, it was noted long ago in another brain area (IT cortex) that the distribution of firing
rates is well approximated by a truncated Gaussian distribution S7.

Derivation of Gaussian process input to CA1 cells. Related to Figure 1.
Here we illustrate how random summation of presynaptic inputs into CA1 cells leads to Gaussian
statistics of the total synaptic input, and elucidate how the spatial correlation function of the
Gaussian process is determined by the input statistics. We assume, for simplicity, completely
independent weights, yet Gaussian statistics can arise also under broader conditions, in which
there is partial correlation between groups of summed variables that decays sufficiently rapidly
across a functional axis within the input population S8.
Consider a population i = 1 . . . N of spatially selective neurons presynpatic to a CA1 place
cell with bounded spatial response function ui(x) for x ∈RD in D dimensions. For simplicity we
consider these response functions to have zero mean, whereas in a more realistic setting ui can
stand for the deviation of the firing rate from its mean, and approximate balancing of the mean
(which is interchangeable also with the choice of threshold in our model) may be obtained by
other mechanisms, such as mutual lateral inhibition or homeostatic regulation of excitatory and
inhibitory inputs.

Convergence to Rayleigh distribution. Related to Figures 1, 2 and 5.
The field size distribution in our model has a stereotypical skewed form that converges to a
Rayleigh distribution in the high threshold limit. To understand this behavior on very qualitative
terms, we note that for high threshold crossings the fields are small compared to the correlation
length of the Gaussian process, and the consecutive threshold crossings demarcating the fields
are correlated (contrast this with field gaps, which in the high threshold limit are large compared
to the correlation length of the process. The consecutive threshold crossings demarcating gaps
are therefore uncorrelated in the high threshold limit, thereby leading to the observed exponential
distribution).
A full mathematical characterization of the threshold crossing’s joint distribution – when the
crossings are nearby as in the case of the place fields, is still an open problem for general
Gaussian processes, yet some results are available. Under broad regularity conditions, it has
been mathematically established that, when the threshold crossings are nearby, the threshold
crossings tend to repel each other S13. Moreover, it is established that the probability of small
distance δx between the consecutive nearby threshold crossing p(δx) behaves, for small δx as
p(δx) ∝δx.
(S19)
Effectively this implies that the probability of small field size approaches 0 when δx →0. The
repulsion between consecutive crossings can be understood intuitively from the observation that
12

for there to be another threshold crossing after a threshold crossing, the derivative of the process
must change sign. Since we assume a smooth process, the sign change of the derivative will only
occur after some distance from the first threshold crossing, effectively suppressing the probability
