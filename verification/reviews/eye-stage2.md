## Required local changes

### EYE-S2-01: teach what gain means before relying on it

Passages: line 21, “an incorrect gain during drift would change the inferred common variance”; line 167, “enters both signals with unit gain.” Earlier path checked: Foundations line 90 mentions “measurement gains” but does not explain the term; Chapters 2–3 do not teach the control/signal meaning. The reader is new to this subject.

Deletion test: removing the calibration-scale assumption loses a condition central to the covariance estimate, so retain it. The word “gain” is useful when connecting the filter's area to its response scale, but it cannot carry an unexplained condition at line 21. Explain that it is the conversion scale between a neural change and a position change, or give that meaning directly in the sentence. At line 167 say explicitly that “unit gain” means the same common displacement enters both signals with the same size. This can replace the existing phrase rather than add a paragraph. The kernel-area calculation at lines 101–118 then gives the concrete numerical example of such a conversion scale. If the technical term is retained as a newly taught term, mark its first teaching occurrence using the established definition style.

### EYE-S2-02: state the coinciding-time-constant case directly

Passage: line 109, “For a repeated pole, r^2=4km, the continuous limit is ...”. “Pole” has not been taught in the earlier path or this chapter and requires control/transform vocabulary that this calculation does not otherwise need.

Deletion test: retain the limiting case. Without it, the displayed double-exponential expression has a zero denominator when its two decay times coincide. Delete the specialist label and say, “When the two decay times coincide, r^2=4km, the limit is ...”. Preserve the formula, condition and the following statement about the fitted distinct-time responses. This is a simpler sufficient explanation of a real mathematical case, not a synonym preference.

### EYE-S2-03: identify the factor as CV squared

Passage: line 451, “with the regularity factor and fixed geometry”. The relevant “regularity factor” is CV^2 from lines 431–443, but the phrase does not identify it. Earlier Chapter 3 also uses “regularity” for conditions on the likelihood, a different meaning.

Deletion test: deleting the factor or the fixed-network comparison would lose assumptions of the scaling argument. Retain them and connect them to the actual derivation. A sufficient local form is “including the CV^2 factor just derived and holding the network geometry fixed”. Keep the distinction between a scaling comparison and a universal equality. No additional glossary term called “regularity factor” is needed.

### EYE-S2-04: give the statistical labels their needed meanings

Passages: line 51, “one sided test ... p=...”; line 120, “standard error of the mean (SEM) ... one sided p=...”. The earlier reading path teaches probability, variance and correlation, but neither the role of a one-sided p value nor what SEM measures is taught there. Expanding SEM supplies its words but not its distinction from cell-to-cell spread.

Deletion test: the numerical evidence is useful for interpreting a weak relationship, so do not remove it just to avoid defining it. Add a short first-use explanation that the one-sided test compares the positive mean correlation with a zero-mean comparison, and that p is the probability, under that comparison, of a test statistic at least as large as observed. At the R mean in line 120, explain that SEM estimates uncertainty in the across-cell mean, not the spread of individual cells or the uncertainty across animals. This does not require teaching the full t-test calculation. Preserve the two-animal qualification already given in line 19 and the distinct weighted-error interpretation in line 222.
