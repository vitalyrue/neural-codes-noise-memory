1  Patterns of variable spikes can represent a value

Nerve cells communicate with brief electrical spikes. This chapter explains how firing rates across a group of cells can represent an eye angle or an animal’s location, and how variable spike timing affects that representation. These ideas supply the biological and probability background for the three papers.

The eyes can hold a position after a brief movement command has ended. An animal can also keep track of its location while the activity of individual neurons fluctuates. Both abilities require a useful quantity to remain represented in a changing biological system. The three papers study different parts of this problem: the loss of a stored value, the origin of slow eye motion, and the arrangement of spatial firing patterns.

Before asking how a circuit keeps a value, we need to know how its activity represents that value and what makes the activity variable. We begin with the spikes that cells send and the patterns of firing rates across a population. A model of spike counts then shows why one brief observation can be uncertain and how combining counts from several cells can help. We then learn how to tell whether measurements vary together and how to separate random spreading from a change in the average value. Those distinctions will let us interpret both a memory model and measurements of eye motion.

1.1 Patterns of firing rates represent a value

A neuron communicates through brief electrical events called spikes. A firing rate is the expected number of spikes per unit time. A rate of 20 hertz means an average of 20 spikes per second under the specified conditions, rather than a promise of one spike every 50 milliseconds.

Neurons affect one another through connections called synapses. In the models used here, inputs have two contrasting effects:

  • An excitatory input increases a receiving neuron’s tendency to fire.
  • An inhibitory input reduces it.

The effect of a spike can last beyond the spike itself because synaptic input decays over time. When neurons send signals back into the same population, the circuit has recurrent connectivity. Such feedback can maintain activity after an external signal disappears.

A tuning curve gives a neuron’s expected firing rate as a function of a represented variable. For eye angle \(\theta\), a simple local model is \(r_i(\theta)=b_i+k_i\theta\). Neuron \(i\) has baseline rate \(b_i\) in spikes per second and slope \(k_i\) in spikes per second per degree. The linear expression can describe an active range, but it must be modified where it would predict negative firing. The eye paper includes a firing threshold and terms for movement as well as position.1

Different cells have different tuning curves. The vector

\[ \mathbf r(\theta)=\bigl(r_1(\theta),\ldots,r_N(\theta)\bigr) \]

is a population code: the pattern across \(N\) neurons carries information about \(\theta\). The position of a cell in this vector is a neuron identity, rather than a location in physical space. Reading a value from observed spikes is decoding. Establishing a population pattern from an input is encoding. The 2012 paper asks how accurately the network can maintain such a pattern using its own noisy activity.

Spatial tuning gives a second example. In the hippocampus, a brain structure involved in memory and navigation, a place cell fires strongly in one or more localized regions of an environment. A connected region of elevated spatial firing is a place field. One neuron can have several fields. A rate map estimates the rate at each position by dividing the spikes recorded there by the time spent there. Sparse visits, smoothing, and firing cutoffs therefore affect the estimated map and its fields. A field in a model’s expected rate and a field extracted from finite recordings are related objects with different measurement uncertainties.2

The hippocampal areas CA3 and CA1 contain place cells, but their connections differ:

  • CA3 supplies major input to CA1 and has substantial recurrent excitation.
  • CA1 also receives input from entorhinal cortex and has much less local recurrent excitation.

A grid cell, found in entorhinal cortex, has repeated spatial fields that often form an approximately hexagonal pattern in a two-dimensional arena. These repeated grid fields and a place cell’s irregular multiple fields should be kept distinct. A model explaining CA1’s output maps need not identify where the animal’s position estimate is maintained.3

1.2 A variable spike count at a fixed rate

A tuning curve describes expected activity, but a brief spike observation can differ each time the same value is represented. The 2012 model describes this variability with a Poisson process. At constant rate \(r\), the number \(K\) of spikes in an interval of length \(\Delta t\) obeys

\[ \Pr(K=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}, \qquad \lambda=r\Delta t, \qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots . \]

Here \(\lambda\) is an expected count, so it has no units. For this distribution,

\[ \mathbb E[K]=\lambda, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(K)=\lambda. \]

Derivation: why a Poisson count has variance equal to its mean

Summing \(k\Pr(K=k)\) gives \(\lambda\). Likewise, summing \(k(k-1)\Pr(K=k)\) gives \(\lambda^2\). Since \(K^2=K(K-1)+K\),

\[ \mathbb E[K^2]=\lambda^2+\lambda, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(K)=\mathbb E[K^2]-\mathbb E[K]^2=\lambda. \]

The count variance grows with rate, while its size relative to the mean falls. Its relative standard deviation is \(\sqrt\lambda/\lambda=1/\sqrt\lambda\). This is why collecting more independent spikes can improve a rate estimate. It also means that increasing a firing-rate scale does not keep absolute noise amplitude fixed. Comparisons between networks must specify which quantities are held constant.4

Example: an apparently silent cell and an informative population

Suppose the expected rate is 20 spikes per second and an observation lasts 50 ms. The expected count is \(20\times0.05=1\). The probability of seeing no spike is \(e^{-1}=0.368\). A silent interval is therefore common even though the expected rate is positive.

Now suppose 20 conditionally independent cells each have that rate. Their total count is Poisson with mean 20 and standard deviation \(\sqrt{20}=4.47\). The total rate estimate has a relative standard deviation of about 22.4%, compared with 100% for one cell. Population averaging helps because the independent count variances add while the mean signal adds directly. If a common varying input makes several cells’ firing rates rise and fall together, that shared variation will not disappear in the same way.

The two count distributions can be compared on the same rate per cell scale.

Dividing the pooled rate estimate by 20 keeps the expected rate per cell fixed, while independent averaging reduces its relative spread. See Poisson count likelihood, SI Eq. S43, p. 9.

For changing rates, the expected count is the integral \(\lambda=\int r(t)\,dt\). In a recurrent network, \(r(t)\) itself depends on past activity. The useful independence statement is therefore conditional: given the current modeled state, the elementary spike-generation noise is independent across cells in the basic 2012 model. Averaging over the changing state can still produce correlations across time and across neurons. This qualification prevents treating a recurrent Poisson network as a collection of independent complete spike trains.

Two other measurements describe different aspects of variability:

  • The coefficient of variation, or CV, of interspike intervals, the times between successive spikes, is their standard deviation divided by their mean. A homogeneous Poisson process has exponential intervals and CV equal to one. More regular firing can have CV below one, as in the ocular motor neurons analyzed in 2022.
  • The count variance divided by mean count is the Fano factor. Its value also depends on the counting interval.

CV and Fano factor cannot be substituted for one another without additional assumptions.

1.3 Shared neural signals and a biased command leave different signs in eye motion

During fixation, a monkey tries to keep looking at a stationary target, yet its eye moves slightly. A changing command from the brain can make many motor neurons change their firing together. Their spikes act on the eye muscles, which turn the eye. An individual motor neuron also adds spike variability of its own. The 2022 paper asks how much of the observed movement follows a shared neural command, and whether that command itself wanders over time.5

Covariance asks which recorded changes occur together. Displacement statistics ask how far the eye moves and how that movement grows with elapsed time. Neither measurement by itself identifies the neural circuit responsible.

Physical meaning: a shared command can survive averaging across neurons. If a common input raises the firing of many motor neurons that pull the eye in the same direction, their effects add. Independent extra and missing spikes partly cancel when those neurons act together. The paper compares the measured eye displacement with a displacement predicted from one neuron’s spikes, after converting both to degrees. That prediction is an analysis of a recording, not an extra command sent to the muscles. A relationship between the two is evidence to test against a model of shared input and private noise. It does not mean that the one recorded cell causes all the movement.

Covariance measures whether two signals tend to be above their own averages together or on opposite sides of those averages. Positive covariance between the two displacements means that above-average changes in the neural prediction tend to accompany above-average changes in eye position. The correlation coefficient, written \(\rho\), expresses this shared variation relative to the spread of each signal. Extra private noise can lower \(\rho\) even when the shared drive has not weakened. The eye-drift calculation explains why a modest correlation need not mean a small shared contribution to movement.

Mathematical definitions: covariance, variance and correlation

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be the two displacements, in degrees, across repeated intervals of the same duration. Subtracting each average leaves its fluctuation. Their covariance is

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E[(X-\mathbb E[X])(Y-\mathbb E[Y])]. \]

Here it has units degrees squared. In general its units are the product of the two signals’ units. Variance is \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,X)\). Expanding the square of a centered sum or difference gives

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X\pm Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)\pm2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y). \]

For nonzero variances, correlation is

\[ \rho_{XY}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)\operatorname{Var}(Y)}}. \]

It is dimensionless and lies between \(-1\) and \(1\). Independence gives zero covariance when these moments exist. Zero covariance alone does not imply independence.

To see the physical roles, write \(X=g_X S+\epsilon_X\) and \(Y=g_Y S+\epsilon_Y\). Here \(S\) is a shared displacement signal in degrees, the dimensionless gains \(g_X,g_Y\) multiply its contribution in each recording, and \(\epsilon_X,\epsilon_Y\) are private contributions in degrees. If all three random contributions are independent,

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=g_Xg_Y\operatorname{Var}(S),\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=g_X^2\operatorname{Var}(S)+\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_X). \]

Increasing private variance increases the denominator of \(\rho\) without increasing the covariance. Changing a gain changes the measured covariance even if the shared source is unchanged. This simple construction explains the need for calibration and explicit noise assumptions. It is not the paper’s full inference model.

Physical meaning: random wandering differs from a persistent directional error. Let \(x(t)\) be horizontal eye angle in degrees and let \(\ell\) be the time between two measurements, called the lag, in seconds. The displacement \(\Delta x=x(t+\ell)-x(t)\) is the change in that angle. A random change in the maintained command can move the eye left in one interval and right in another. The average displacement can then be close to zero while individual displacements are large. A persistent imbalance in the command can instead make the average displacement grow in one direction. Muscles smooth the command before it becomes eye movement, so the timescale of the observation also matters.

The mean squared displacement, or MSD, averages \((\Delta x)^2\) across repeated intervals and has units degrees squared. Its two contributions are

\[ \mathbb E[(\Delta x)^2]=\operatorname{Var}(\Delta x)+\mathbb E[\Delta x]^2. \]

A larger MSD can mean stronger random movement, a larger directional bias, or both. Measuring the mean displacement separately prevents mistaking bias for increased noise.

Derivation: separating spread from mean displacement

Write \(\mu=\mathbb E[\Delta x]\) and \(\eta=\Delta x-\mu\), so \(\mathbb E[\eta]=0\). Squaring and averaging gives

\[ \mathbb E[(\mu+\eta)^2]=\mu^2+2\mu\mathbb E[\eta]+\mathbb E[\eta^2] =\mu^2+\operatorname{Var}(\Delta x). \]

The cross term vanishes because the fluctuations are measured relative to their own mean. This identity needs finite second moments, but no diffusion assumption.

What changes the physical movement? The model must say which part of the pathway a parameter controls:

  • Random changes in the stored command: more frequent or larger lasting changes make the stored angle spread faster. The next chapter calls this rate of spreading the diffusion coefficient \(D\) and explains why it depends on spike variability and the circuit’s response to each spike.
  • A persistent directional imbalance: this changes the mean displacement. In a constant-velocity approximation, doubling that velocity doubles the mean displacement at a fixed lag and quadruples its squared contribution to MSD.
  • The gain from command to eye angle: multiplying the command’s contribution to each displacement by a gain \(G\) multiplies that contribution’s variance and MSD by \(G^2\), with the input fluctuations held fixed. Any noise added after that gain needs to be counted separately. A fitted tuning slope, which describes rate as a function of angle, is not by itself a causal muscle gain.
  • Muscle response time: a slower response smooths rapid command changes more strongly. It changes the short-lag shape of the MSD curve, not just its height. The timing chapter separates this smoothing from growth of noise inside the memory circuit.

Measurement error is a separate contribution: it changes recorded positions without moving the eye. Independent zero-mean position errors, each of variance \(\sigma_m^2\) and independent of true motion, add \(2\sigma_m^2\) to displacement MSD at nonzero lags. Thus an upward shift in the observed curve need not be a physical increase in movement. The measurement-noise calculation shows the conditions needed to remove it.
