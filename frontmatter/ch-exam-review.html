Results and connections across the three papers

Return after studying the three papers to reconstruct their arguments, locate a formula, read its symbols and check its assumptions. Core results are visible; supporting results remain in closed notes.

Each paper studies a different physical system. This overview connects what changes in each system, what the measurements can establish, and the formulas that express the results. The later chapters develop the underlying arguments.

Three questions organize the three papers

Common quantities and notation. The same physical or mathematical quantity can have different letters in the three papers. Use these meanings throughout the review; the paper keys supply the model-specific details.

  • Firing rate: \(r_i\) in the memory model, \(f\) or \(\lambda\) in the eye model, and \(F(x)\) in the place-field model. All are expected spike counts per unit time, in spikes/s or Hz.
  • Coordinate: \(\theta\) labels the current value represented by the memory circuit; \(E\) is the true physical eye angle in degrees; \(x,y\) are physical positions in the place-field model, scalar on a track or vectors in space. The memory coordinate has the chosen model’s units, for example radians; place-field positions have distance units. Each coordinate keeps the physical meaning and units of its own model.
  • Indices and counts: \(i,j\) label cells in neural weight and rate arrays; E1 instead uses \(j\) to label spikes, and P11 uses \(j\) for geometric dimension. \(N\) is a dimensionless count: neurons in M1–M16 and E7, but fields in P5 and P12. \(N_m\) specifically counts motor neurons.
  • Time and response times: time intervals and decay times have seconds as units. \(t\) is time in the temporal models; \(\tau\), \(\tau_s\), \(\tau_f\) and \(\tau_\pm\) label particular response or decay times. Their biological roles differ and are specified locally. P9 instead uses \(t\) as a coordinate along a spatial line.
  • Diffusion coefficient: \(D\) in the 2012 and 2022 models has units of the evolving coordinate squared per second. The unfiltered diffusing coordinate has displacement variance \(2Dt\) for constant \(D\) without drift or boundaries; a filtered eye output need not have that MSD at short lags. Here \(d\), used for spatial dimension, is a different quantity.
  • Information rate: \(J\), where used in the memory model and the eye scaling estimate, is information per second about the represented coordinate, in inverse coordinate units squared per second. The main argument uses scalar \(D,J\); M5 and M11 use matrices.
  • Estimates and arrays: a hat marks an estimate or prediction, as in \(\hat\theta\) or \(\widehat E\); the row identifies its observations. Bold symbols denote vectors or matrices, and a superscript \(T\) transposes an array.

Letters whose meanings change. Check the argument, subscript and paper before identifying a symbol with another occurrence:

  • \(r\): a firing rate in the memory model; the velocity coefficient in the eye calibration equation E1. Eye firing rates there use \(f\) or \(\lambda\).
  • \(W\): connection weights in the memory model, a Brownian process in E6, and random input weights \(W_i\) in P2.
  • \(h\): a counting-window duration in memory calculations, an eye-response kernel \(h(u)\), or the spatial input map \(h(x)\).
  • \(C\): shared displacement in E3; \(C_E\) is eye-position covariance; \(C(x,y)\) is spatial input covariance. Covariance units are the product of the input variables’ units, while displacement has angle units.
  • \(s\): synaptic activation \(s_i\), a time lag or integration time in some temporal calculations, or field size in the place-field model.
  • \(\chi\): the shared movement-variance fraction in E3; \(\chi_d\) is the signed Euler-characteristic count in P11–P12.
  • \(\sigma\) and \(V\): the place-field chapters use \(\sigma\) for distance scale \(\ell_c\), whereas \(\sigma_0\) is input standard deviation and the eye model’s \(\sigma_m\) is measurement-error standard deviation. \(V\) denotes error variance in memory rows, but environment size in P12.

Feedback sustains activity while noisy spikes move the remembered value

Burak and Fiete (2012): continuous memory in a modeled neural circuit. The stored value is a coordinate of a persistent neural activity pattern. Fixed recurrent connections sustain that pattern. Random spikes disturb it; the circuit removes some disturbances, while others leave the value displaced. The derivation follows that surviving displacement and asks how its variance relates to how clearly the spikes distinguish nearby values.

Recall the chain: feedback mechanism (M1) → surviving displacement (M3) → spike information (M7) → diffusion bound and equality (M8–M9) → readout (M13). Long-delay storage error and total recall error are M6 and M14 in the additional results below.

Primes on rate and input tuning curves denote derivatives with respect to the represented value. In M9, \(\phi'\) differentiates the transfer function with respect to its input. The shared key below defines recurring symbols; rows list only additions and exceptions. Diffusion and Fisher information are scalars in the main argument; M5 gives their matrix generalization for several stored coordinates.

Memory-specific additions. Use the common notation for rates, coordinates, time units, \(D\) and \(J\). These additions apply to both memory tables; a row states any further change or extra symbol.

  • \(W_{ij}\) sends activation from cell \(j\) to receiver \(i\).
  • \(\Theta\): the initial represented value. \(h\): short counting window; \(T\): storage delay; \(L\): observation duration after the recall cue. The source writes readout duration as \(\Delta T\).
  • \(s_i\): outgoing activation of cell \(i\), dimensionless with one unit added per spike. Bars in \(\bar s_i\) and \(\bar r_i\) denote noise-free steady values.
  • \(\bar r_i'=d\bar r_i/d\theta\): tuning slope, in rate units per coordinate unit.
  • \(g_i\): total input; \(b_i\): constant bias (same input units). \(W\) is an \(N\times N\) matrix in input units per activation unit. \(\phi(g)\) converts input into rate; \(\phi'\) differentiates with respect to input.
  • \(\tau\): synaptic activation decay time.
  • \(K\): \(N\times N\) linear response matrix (s\(^{-1}\)). \(\mathbf u=\partial\bar{\mathbf s}/\partial\theta\): tangent \(N\)-vector (activation/coordinate). \(\mathbf v\): normalized left zero vector; \(v_i\) measures the retained coordinate shift per activation unit from cell \(i\).
  • \(V\) with a subscript denotes error variance (coordinate units squared); the subscript identifies the estimate being assessed.

Recall check: why can persistent activity still lose the original value?

The circuit can restore the shape of its activity pattern without restoring its original coordinate along the family of patterns. The left projection weights measure the displacement that remains. Fisher information concerns reading the present value from spikes; diffusion concerns how that present value has departed from the original cue. A decoder can therefore read the present state precisely while the original memory is already inaccurate.

Covariance identifies a shared component of eye movement

Ben-Shushan et al. (2022): horizontal eye drift during fixation in macaque monkeys. A recorded motor neuron’s spikes are converted into a prediction in degrees. Comparing that prediction with actual displacement asks how much variation they share. A second argument asks whether independent motor noise, after muscle filtering, could explain the measured mean squared displacement (MSD).

Recall the chain: calibrate the spike-to-movement filter (E1) → estimate the shared variance fraction (E3) → compare filtered stationary noise with an accumulating command (E5–E6). E6 is a simplified calculation developed here, not the paper’s full circuit model. The separate population bound (E2) challenges independent motor noise alone; it is in the additional results below.

The time lag in an MSD is the separation between two readings. The eye response time in E6 is different from the synaptic decay time in the 2012 theory. Measurement correction and parameter scaling remain available below the core argument.

Eye-specific additions. Use the common notation for firing rates, angle units, time, \(D\) and estimate hats. These additions apply to both eye tables; filter normalization and particular response times are specified in their rows.

  • \(\ell\): time separation between two readings; \(u\): integration lag. A dot differentiates with respect to time.
  • \(\widehat E\): angle predicted from spikes; \(E_{\rm obs}\): measured angle.
  • \(M(\ell)=\mathbb E[(E(t+\ell)-E(t))^2]\): mean squared displacement (MSD), in degrees\(^2\). \(C_E(\ell)\): covariance of centered eye position at lag \(\ell\), also in degrees\(^2\).
  • \(h\): causal response kernel. Its units depend on the input normalization in E1 or E5; it is not the spatial input map in the place-field model.
  • \(R_i\): correlation between cell \(i\)’s displacement prediction and measured displacement over the same 350 ms intervals, dimensionless. \(R\) omits the cell index.
More eye results: the independent-noise bound, measurement error, and parameter scaling
Recall check: why does weak single-cell correlation not imply a small shared contribution?

Private spike noise can make one cell’s prediction much noisier than the movement. Correlation divides covariance by both signals’ standard deviations, so the noisy predictor lowers it. The calibrated shared-fraction estimate instead divides covariance by measured displacement variance. This interpretation needs the gain and noise assumptions; locating the source upstream also needs a biological premise about private motor-neuron variability.

Thresholded Gaussian input links spatial structure to place-field statistics

Mainali, Azeredo da Silveira, and Burak (2025): place fields in rodent and bat recordings, compared with a spatial statistical model. A cell’s input is a correlated Gaussian map over position. Once drawn, that map stays fixed during traversal. Thresholding it makes connected active regions. Crossings determine 1D counts and mean lengths; isolated high peaks supply the limiting size laws in different dimensions. Slices and holes test spatial structure beyond a size histogram.

Recall the chain: input map and threshold (P1) → exact crossings and mean-length constraints (P3–P4) → high-threshold size law (P6). Slice and topology tests (P9 and P11) probe spatial structure further; they are in the additional results below.

The map varies over space, unlike the memory coordinate that diffuses over time. The local distance scale is written as \(\ell_c\) here; the place-field chapters call the same scale \(\sigma\). It is distinct from input standard deviation \(\sigma_0\).

Place-field-specific additions. Use the common notation for physical position, firing rate and the distinction between spatial and temporal variables. These additions apply to both place-field tables.

  • \(d\): spatial dimension, 1, 2 or 3. Positions are in \(\mathbb R^d\).
  • \(h(x)\): fixed Gaussian input map; \(\mu\): constant input mean; \(\sigma_0\): input standard deviation; \(h_{\rm th}\): absolute firing threshold (all in input units).
  • \(z=(h-\mu)/\sigma_0\): normalized input; \(q=(h_{\rm th}-\mu)/\sigma_0\): normalized threshold; \(\epsilon\): peak excess above \(q\) (all dimensionless).
  • \(a\): gain converting normalized excess into firing rate (Hz). It is not the eye model’s tuning slope.
  • \(C(x,y)\), or \(C(y-x)\) for a stationary map: covariance of unnormalized input, in input units squared. It is not the eye model’s shared displacement \(C\). Spatial primes differentiate with respect to distance.
  • \(\ell_c\): local distance scale, written \(\sigma\) in the place-field chapters; distinct from input standard deviation \(\sigma_0\).
  • \(Q(q)=\Pr(Z>q)\) for a standard normal scalar \(Z\): dimensionless Gaussian upper-tail probability. \(\nu_+(q)\): upcrossing density (distance\(^{-1}\)).
  • \(s\): complete-field size (length, area or volume, with distance\(^d\) units); \(S\): nonnegative cutoff in the same units.
  • \(v_d\): dimensionless unit-ball volume, with \(v_1=2\), \(v_2=\pi\) and \(v_3=4\pi/3\). \(\beta\): size-law coefficient, in inverse distance squared in every dimension.

Recall check: why do exact crossing counts not make the size law exact?

The crossing calculation averages height and slope at threshold under the smooth stationary Gaussian model. It does not require high threshold. The universal size argument additionally replaces a typical field by an isolated nearly quadratic high peak and uses the limiting peak-excess distribution. At finite threshold a field can have several peaks or an irregular shape. The extra approximation has to be checked, even when the crossing formula is exact.

Different quantities answer different questions

Before giving a result, check which of these questions it answers:

  • Activity, stored value or estimate? Neural activations form the circuit state; a coordinate identifies the represented value; a decoder computes an estimate from observations. Persistent activity alone does not guarantee accurate memory. M1, M3, M13.
  • Present-state precision or storage loss? Fisher information measures a local distinction in present spike probabilities. Diffusion measures accumulated movement of the stored value. M7–M8.
  • Correlation or a causal variance fraction? A shared-fraction interpretation needs calibrated gains and a noise model. Correlation alone does not supply it. E3.
  • Internal command or observed movement? Muscle response and feedback change how internal fluctuations appear in eye motion. E5–E6.
  • Temporal noise or a fixed spatial map? The 2012 and 2022 arguments concern evolving signals. The 2025 spatial randomness describes an ensemble of possible maps, not fresh input at each revisit. M6, P1.
  • Exact model identity or limiting approximation? Gaussian crossing and topology formulas have their stated exact model scope. Universal field-size laws need a high threshold; nearly independent field locations need sufficiently decaying spatial dependence as well. P3, P6, P4 and its supporting rare-event result.
