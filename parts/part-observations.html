Part II: Eye motion and place fields constrain different parts of a neural code

Unreviewed draft diagram. This AI-generated image may contain incorrect or missing labels, arrows, formulas and numbers. Use the Part text and paper links as the source for the explanation.

These studies connect neural activity to eye control in monkeys and location in rodents and bats. Ben-Shushan et al. (2022) supply the central shared-drive inference and a possible integrator explanation in Chapters 7 to 9. Mainali et al. (2025) supply the spatial model, field-size predictions, and supporting shape tests in Chapters 10 to 12. Chapter 13 is a comparison developed here of those results with Burak and Fiete (2012). The eye chapters begin with recordings and then test a possible neural source. The place chapters begin with a spatial input model and then test its field statistics.

The contribution labels describe the role of each result here. Central is a main finding of the cited paper. Supporting establishes or qualifies that finding. Background supplies needed concepts. Developed here identifies an explanation, comparison, or proposed test developed here.

Chapters in this part

  • Chapter 7: Eye drift points to a shared neural drive

    Central empirical result: Ben-Shushan et al. (2022) infer a substantial shared drive upstream of ocular motor neurons from recorded eye motion and spikes. The filter and covariance calculations explain the assumptions behind that inference.

  • Chapter 8: Drift growth distinguishes diffusion from filtered white noise

    Supporting test: Ben-Shushan et al. (2022) compare displacement statistics with the predictions of mechanical filtering and private motor noise. The one-filter calculation is a teaching model developed here, distinct from the full simulation.

  • Chapter 9: A noisy memory circuit can produce eye drift

    Central proposed explanation: Ben-Shushan et al. (2022) show that a noisy integrator model can account for the motion after muscle filtering and delayed feedback. Aksay et al. (2007) supply supporting circuit architecture. The match supports a possible source, rather than uniquely identifying it.

  • Chapter 10: Field counts and gaps reveal threshold and scale

    Central spatial model: Mainali et al. (2025) describe fields and gaps as regions of a smooth random input above or below threshold. Expanded crossing and parameter-inference calculations are developed here.

  • Chapter 11: High peaks explain size laws across dimensions

    Central size predictions: Mainali et al. (2025) derive dimension-dependent field-size statistics and compare them with recordings. This book expands the local-peak derivation and separates limiting laws from finite-threshold behavior.

  • Chapter 12: Holes and measurements test the spatial explanation

    Supporting tests and circuit interpretation: Mainali et al. (2025) examine shapes, slices, and connectedness beyond the fitted summaries. The underlying recordings include Eliav et al. (2021), Harland et al. (2021), and Yartsev and Ulanovsky (2013). Gaussian maps constrain possible inputs without uniquely identifying wiring.

  • Chapter 13: Spatial maps and memory dynamics need separate tests

    Synthesis: The comparison joins the questions asked by Burak and Fiete (2012), Ben-Shushan et al. (2022), and Mainali et al. (2025). Its common-displacement calculation and proposed joint experiment are developed here. The three papers did not jointly test that model.
